A Ladder of Hybrids

The two ideal branches of the security game differ in every challenge answer at once: one hands out recorded keys, the other random ones. M7 builds a ladder of 2q + 2 games between them, each rung changing the answer at one more epoch, and proves that the bottom rung is one branch and the top rung is the other. The adversary's advantage is then at most q · εcor plus half the sum of the rung-to-rung gaps. This page explains the ladder, its two endpoints, and the one hypothesis the proof still owes.

01

From two ideal branches to a sum of small gaps

After M5 and N, the adversary's guessing advantage against Opp-BiKEM is bounded by q · εcor plus half the distance between the two ideal branches: the ideal game that answers challenges with recorded keys, Ifalse, and the one that answers with random keys, Itrue. Bounding that distance is the cryptographic heart of the proof, and it cannot be done in one step: a single KEM challenge can be planted at one epoch, not at all of them.

This pageM7: the epoch hybrids, their exact endpoints at thresholds 0 and above 2q, and the telescoped bound that reduces the proof to one per-epoch hop.
RemainingM8, M10, M11: each positive-epoch hop is at most the IND-CPA advantage of an explicit reduction. Stage A of this is done; see the next tutorial.
02

Why hybrids: one epoch at a time

A reduction to the KEM's IND-CPA security gets one challenge: one public key, one ciphertext, one candidate key. It can embed that challenge at one epoch of the simulated protocol. So a reduction can only ever relate two games that differ in the challenge answer of a single epoch. The two ideal branches differ at every epoch the adversary might challenge.

The standard remedy is a chain of intermediate games. Let HT be the ideal game in which challenges at epochs below T are answered with random keys and all others with the recorded key. Then

H0 = Ifalse and HT ≈ Itrue for every T > 2q The first is a definitional equality of computations. The second is an equality of output distributions, for adversaries making at most q sends.

and adjacent games Ht, Ht+1 differ only in the answer at epoch t, which is what one KEM challenge can handle. The triangle inequality turns the distance between the ends into a sum of adjacent distances. Everything in M7 is about defining the ladder so that each rung is a well-behaved game and proving the two endpoint statements.

Why the top rung is the random-key game

The bottom equality is immediate: at threshold 0 no epoch is randomised, so every answer is the recorded key. The top equality deserves a closer look, because HT does not randomise every epoch; it randomises the epochs below T and answers all others with the recorded key. Compare the two games one challenge at a time.

A challenge of epoch e in HT and in Itrue, for T > 2q
EpochHT would answerItrue would answerDo they agree?

So the epochs at or above the threshold are the only ones where the two games could come apart, and the argument is that they never get the chance: a challenge there is rejected in both games before either consults its answering rule. The epochs below the threshold need no argument at all. This is why the explanation is about epochs above 2q, not below.

The fact doing the work is the epoch counter from M4′. Each party's epoch advances by two per key-generating send, A's keys sit at even epochs and B's at odd ones, and together they commit at most one advance per send. With at most q sends in a run, the largest epoch at which either table can ever hold a key is 2q. Choosing T > 2q places every keyed epoch below the threshold, where HT already answers randomly. The ladder of 2q + 1 hops therefore reaches a game indistinguishable from Itrue.

The same reasoning explains the extra rung at the bottom. Epoch 0 is never keyed either, so H0 and H1, which differ only at epoch 0, both reject every challenge of it and agree. The exact hops are the ones at epochs that are never keyed; every other hop is a genuine cryptographic step.

03 · implemented

The epoch-indexed challenge oracle

The generic SCKA challenge oracle takes a single Boolean: random answers or recorded answers, for every epoch. M7 adds a selection-indexed version in the generic security file:

oracleChallOn (random : ℕ → Bool) := fun t => oracleChall (random t) t

At epoch t it is literally the old oracle with bit random t. Nothing else changes: the same eligibility test (not exposed, not challenged), the same table lookup with A's table first, the same rejection when no key is recorded, the same insertion into the challenged set. A constant selection is the old oracle, by rfl.

The Opp-BiKEM hybrid with selection random is the ideal security game with this oracle in place of the fixed-bit one:

hybridSecurityImpl random :=
  securityImplOf (idealCorrectnessImpl …)        -- ideal sends, ideal receives, sampling
                 (oracleChallOn random …)       -- the only change
                 (exposure .A leak) (exposure .B leak) scheme

hybridRandom T := fun t => decide (t < T)        -- random exactly below the threshold
hybridExp T adv := (simulateQ (hybridSecurityImpl (hybridRandom T)) adv).run' initState

Sends, receives, leaking sends, corruptions and the exposure policy are those of the ideal game from M4. In particular, the tables stay honest in every hybrid: a random challenge answer is handed to the adversary but never written into a table. That matters for the top endpoint, where the invariant on tables is what makes every eligible epoch small.

04 · implemented

Every hybrid is locally an ideal game

The roadmap expected the hybrid's invariant preservation and epoch-counter step to be re-proved by a new dispatch over the ten oracles. That was unnecessary, thanks to one observation:

∀ query t, ∃ bit b, ∀ state s: (hybrid t).run s = (idealb t).run s hybridSecurityImpl_query_eq_ideal. The bit is random e for a challenge at epoch e, and arbitrary for every other query.

Every single hybrid query is, as a computation, a fixed-bit ideal query. So anything proved for both fixed-bit ideal games about one query transfers to the hybrid in three lines: the hybrid preserves GameInv (from M4′), and a hybrid query raises the counter advances by at most one on sends and not at all otherwise (from M4′).

Read the quantifiers carefully. The bit is chosen per query, uniformly over states. The lemma does not say a hybrid run is some fixed-bit ideal run; different queries pick different bits. Its consumers only ever use properties that hold for either bit, so this is exactly enough and no more.

One more agreement lemma does the real work at both endpoints. A hybrid query and a fixed-bit ideal query compute the same thing from a state s whenever the selection equals the bit at every epoch eligible in s: not exposed, not challenged, and keyed in one of the tables. At ineligible epochs the challenge oracle rejects regardless of the bit, so the selection is never consulted there.

hybridSecurityImpl_run_eq_ideal random b s
  (hs : ∀ e, eligible s e → random e = b) (t) :
  (hybridSecurityImpl random t).run s = (idealSecurityImpl b t).run s

06 · proved

The bottom endpoint and the exact first hop

At threshold 0 nothing is below the threshold, so the selection is constantly false and the hybrid is the ideal game with bit false. This is an equality of computations, not just of distributions:

hybridExp 0 adv = idealSecurityExp false adv hybridExp_zero: unfold hybridRandom 0, use hybridSecurityImpl_const, and the two sides are the same term.

The first hop, from H0 to H1, changes the answer at epoch 0 only. But epoch 0 is never keyed: the invariant clause key_zero says both tables are empty there, on every reachable state. A challenge of epoch 0 is therefore rejected in both hybrids, and the agreement lemma applies with the invariant GameInv at every query:

Pr[H1 = true] = Pr[H0 = true] probOutput_hybridExp_one. Proved with VCVio's invariant-based simulation equality, invariant GameInv, preserved by M4′.

This is why the final theorem will not need a KEM reduction at target 0: that hop is exactly zero, and the roadmap's sum over t < 2q + 1 can include it harmlessly.

07 · proved

The top endpoint needs the bound at every query, not at the end

Above 2q every challengeable epoch is randomised, so HT should behave like Itrue. The agreement lemma says exactly that for one query from one state, provided every eligible epoch in that state is below T. Eligible means keyed, keyed means at most 2 · advances by M4′, and advances ≤ q < T/2 would finish it.

The catch is in advances ≤ q. M4′ proved it for the final state of a run with at most q sends. The agreement is needed at every query of the run. An adaptive adversary could, in principle, spend its sends early, drive some epoch above the threshold, and challenge it before the run ends. The proof must show that this never happens at any intermediate point.

A budget-indexed invariant

The remedy is to carry the remaining send budget in the invariant. VCVio's query-bound transport lemma compares two oracle implementations along a computation whose queries are bounded by a budget; it asks for agreement and preservation only at queries the budget still permits, and it threads the budget through the run. M7 instantiates it with

Inv s r := GameInv s ∧ advances s + r ≤ q      -- r is the remaining send budget

and the adversary's own send bound SecuritySendQueryBound adv q as the budget witness. That bound counts ordinary and leaking sends by both parties along every path of the adversary's computation, decrementing by one at each send query regardless of its outcome, and forbidding a send when the budget is zero.

The M7 evaluation singled this out as the point an adversarial reader should attack, and confirmed that the budget-indexed invariant closes it: rejected sends still consume budget, receives and challenges cannot raise advances, and the agreement is checked before every permitted query rather than inferred from the final state.

Boundary cases. The theorem requires the strict inequality 2q < T, so the telescope uses T = 2q + 1, which randomises epoch 2q as well. For q = 0 the top is T = 1, the only hop is the exact one at epoch 0, and the per-hop hypothesis below is vacuous.

08 · proved

Telescoping

With both endpoints in hand, the distance between the ideal branches is the distance between the two ends of the ladder, which is at most the sum of the rung-to-rung distances. Mathlib's dist_le_range_sum_dist does this in one line for the sequence T ↦ Pr[HT = true]:

| Pr[Itrue] − Pr[Ifalse] | ≤ Σt < 2q+1 | Pr[Ht+1] − Pr[Ht] | idealDist_le_sum_hybrid_hops. The left side is rewritten with hybridExp_zero and probOutput_hybridExp_top at T = 2q + 1.

Combining with the earlier results takes two more steps. N expressed the guessing advantage as half the distance between the two real branches. M5 placed each real branch within q · εcor of its ideal branch. Two triangle inequalities give

AdvSCKA(A) ≤ q · εcor + ½ · | Pr[Itrue] − Pr[Ifalse] | sckaGuessAdvantage_le_ideal_term. Two uses of the M5 endpoint, each q · εcor, halved: the coefficient is q · εcor, not 2q · εcor.
09 · proved

The theorem, and the one hypothesis it owes

Putting the pieces together, for any non-negative per-epoch bound δ that dominates the positive-epoch hops:

AdvSCKA(A) ≤ q · εcor + ½ Σt < 2q+1 δt sckaGuessAdvantage_le_of_hop_bound, assuming correct erasure codes, SecuritySendQueryBound adv q, 0 ≤ δ t for all t, and the hop hypothesis below for 1 ≤ t ≤ 2q. 
hhop : ∀ t, 0 < t → t ≤ 2 * q →
  |Pr[= true | hybridExp (t + 1) adv] − Pr[= true | hybridExp t adv]| ≤ δ t

In the proof, the summand at t = 0 is handled by the exact first hop and only uses 0 ≤ δ 0; every other summand is the hypothesis. The theorem is honest about what it does not contain: no reduction, no assumption that KEM advantages are small, no unconditional secrecy claim. It converts the security proof into a statement about one epoch at a time.

What the hypothesis asks for

The eventual instantiation is δt := AdvIND-CPAKEM(reductiont) for an explicit reduction, which is automatically non-negative. Proving the hop hypothesis for it is the remaining work: a reduction that embeds the KEM challenge at epoch t, stops when t is exposed, and simulates the two adjacent hybrids in its two branches. The next tutorial explains the first stage of that argument, the cancellation of exposed paths, and the plan for the rest.

One convention to carry forward. In the SCKA game, selection value true means a random answer; in VCVio's IND-CPA experiment, bit true means the real key. So Ht, which hands out the recorded key at t, will pair with the KEM's real branch, and Ht+1 with its random branch. The absolute value in hhop tolerates the reversal; branch-by-branch statements must name it.

Check your understanding

Separate the claims

Is a hybrid a run of some fixed-bit ideal game?

No. Each query of a hybrid is a fixed-bit ideal query, with the bit depending on the epoch challenged. Different queries use different bits, so the run as a whole is neither ideal branch. The per-query equality is enough to transfer per-query facts such as invariant preservation and the counter step.

Why are the tables honest in every hybrid?

Because the challenge oracle hands the random key to the adversary without writing it anywhere. Only sends and receives write tables, and they are the ideal game's. If random answers were recorded, the invariant pinning tables to the transcript would fail, and so would the keyed-epoch bound that the top endpoint relies on.

Why is the final-state counter bound from M4′ not enough for the top endpoint?

The agreement of a hybrid query with the Itrue query must hold at every query of the run, since the run is compared query by query. A final-state bound says nothing about intermediate states. Carrying the remaining send budget in the invariant gives advances ≤ q at every point.

Do rejected sends count against the budget?

Yes. The query bound counts send queries, not successful sends. A rejected send consumes one unit of budget and raises advances by zero, which keeps the budget invariant with room to spare.

Why does the sum run to 2q rather than q?

Epochs are counted per party, and each send advances one party's responder epoch by two. With q sends split between the parties, keyed epochs reach at most 2q. The ladder needs one rung per possibly keyed epoch, plus one for epoch 0 which is exact.

Where does the factor ½ come from, and why not 2q · ε?

The factor ½ is N: the guessing advantage is half the distance between the two fixed-bit branches. Each branch is moved from real to ideal at cost q · εcor, for a total of 2q · εcor inside the distance, which the ½ halves back to q · εcor.

Does the theorem already prove Opp-BiKEM secure?

No. It proves a conditional bound: if every positive-epoch hop is at most δt, then the advantage is at most the displayed sum. The hop bound is what M8 to M11 establish, with δt the advantage of an explicit KEM reduction.
